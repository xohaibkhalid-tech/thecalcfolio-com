How Much Car Can You Actually Afford?

Calculate it for your own numbersFree, no sign-up — runs entirely in your browser.
Car Loan Calculator →

A common guideline: keep your total car payment under 15% of your monthly take-home pay, and your total car price under about half your annual income. But the real answer depends on your other debts, down payment, and how long you're willing to finance. Calculate the full monthly cost — payment plus insurance, fuel, and maintenance — before you shop.

Start with the payment, not the price

Most people shop backwards: they find a car they love, then figure out how to pay for it. Flip it. Decide your monthly number first, then find cars that fit. Here's a quick way to get that number:

Take your monthly take-home pay (what actually lands in your account, not your salary). Multiply by 0.15. That's your ceiling for the loan payment alone — not counting insurance, gas, and maintenance, which we'll get to.

Example: $4,500 take-home × 15% = $675/month max for the car payment. At 7% APR over 60 months with $3,000 down, that buys roughly a $33,000 car. Run your own numbers in our car loan calculator — plug in different prices and see what the payment does.

The 15% rule and the 50% rule

Two guidelines the financially sane swear by:

  • 15% rule: your car payment shouldn't exceed 15% of take-home pay. This keeps the loan from crowding out savings and everything else.
  • 50% rule: the car's total price shouldn't exceed about half your gross annual income. Earning $60k? Aim under $30k for the car.

Are these laws? No. But they've survived because they work. People who stretch to 25% of income on a car payment almost always regret it within a year — the payment starts competing with rent, savings, and, you know, having a life.

Reality check: if the payment only works on a 72- or 84-month term, you can't afford that car. The term isn't the problem — the price is.

Don't forget the OTHER costs

This is where budgets die. The payment is just the beginning:

CostTypical monthly hit
Insurance$100–$250+ (higher for new/financed cars — full coverage is usually required)
Fuel$100–$200 depending on commute
Maintenance & repairs$50–$100 averaged out
Registration & taxesVaries by state, budget ~$30–$80/month equivalent

A $500 car payment easily becomes $800+ a month all-in. That's the number to measure against your income, not the payment alone. Newer cars cost more to insure; financed cars require full coverage. Factor it all in before you fall in love with anything.

New vs used: the affordability cheat code

Want to afford "more car"? Buy used. A 2–3 year old car has already taken its biggest depreciation hit — someone else paid for it — but still has most of its reliable life ahead. You get a $35,000 car for $24,000, your payment drops by a third, and your insurance is cheaper too.

The sweet spot for most buyers: 2–4 years old, reasonable mileage, solid reliability record. Let someone else eat the steepest depreciation. Your wallet will notice the difference every single month.

The down payment changes everything

Bigger down payment = smaller loan = lower payment = less interest = you stay above water on value. The classic target is 20%, and it's still the right target. On a $28,000 car, that's $5,600 down.

Can't swing 20%? Then buy a cheaper car, not a bigger loan. I know that's not what you want to hear, but the math doesn't negotiate. A smaller loan on a cheaper car beats a stretched loan on a nicer one every single time.

Quick answers

What salary do I need for a $30,000 car?

Using the 50% guideline, around $60,000 gross annual income. Using the 15% payment rule with $4,500/month take-home, a $30,000 car at typical rates and terms fits — but run your full budget including insurance first.

Is leasing cheaper than buying?

Leasing usually means lower monthly payments but you never own the car — it's essentially a long-term rental with mileage limits. Buying costs more monthly but builds equity. For most people who keep cars 5+ years, buying wins.

How much should I put down on a car?

Aim for 20%. At minimum, put down enough (plus trade-in value) that you won't owe more than the car's worth — going underwater on a car loan is a miserable trap.
