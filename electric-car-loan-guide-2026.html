Financing an Electric Car in 2026: Loans, Credits and Real Costs

The federal $7,500 EV tax credit is gone — it ended for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025. So financing an electric car in 2026 is a different game: the loan rate, your state's remaining rebates, and the total cost of the loan now decide whether the math works. Get those right and an EV can still be a smart buy. Get them wrong and you'll feel it every month.

The $7,500 credit is dead — here's what replaced it

Let's clear this up first, because a lot of old articles floating around Google still say "get your $7,500 credit." That program is over. The federal clean-vehicle credit of up to $7,500 for new EVs and up to $4,000 for used ones ended for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025. The leasing loophole — the commercial credit that let dealers quietly pass $7,500 into lease deals — ended too. And the federal home-charger credit expired for chargers placed in service after June 30, 2026.

So what's left? Honestly, less than people expect, but it's not nothing:

  • The auto loan interest deduction. This is the one remaining federal benefit, and it changed the game. Through 2028, you can deduct up to $10,000 of interest per year on a loan for a new, US-assembled vehicle. It applies to EVs and gas cars alike, and it's subject to income limits — so check the current IRS rules before counting on it. But on a big loan at today's rates, this is real money back in your pocket.
  • State rebates. About 20 states still run some kind of EV incentive, though a few have already run out of funding. The amounts vary wildly: Colorado offers up to $9,000 on a new EV (or $6,000 used) for income-qualified buyers, as a point-of-sale rebate. New York has $2,000, New Jersey $1,500 (up to $4,000 income-qualified), Massachusetts $3,500. Whether you get anything depends entirely on your zip code — look yours up before you shop.
  • Manufacturer financing deals. With the credits gone and sales softening, automakers are discounting to move metal. Tesla was advertising 1.99% APR on most Model Y trims and some Model 3 trims in October 2026 — less than a third of the market average.

Nobody is handing you $7,500 off the sticker anymore. The savings now come from the loan — the rate, the term, and that interest deduction. Financing strategy matters more for EV buyers in 2026 than it did when the credit papered over everything.

What EV loan rates actually look like right now

Here's the thing about EV financing: there's no separate "EV loan rate." You get the same auto loan market as everyone else, and that market is expensive. The average APR on a financed new car sat at about 7.0% in the third quarter of 2026, per Edmunds data, and Bankrate's October 2026 survey showed 60-month new-car loans averaging 7.18%. Used-car loans are steeper still — averaging around 11.2%.

Two EV quirks worth knowing: manufacturer promotional APRs on electric models are currently some of the best rates in the whole market, and some lenders price EVs slightly better because the buyers skew prime. Ask your lender about both.

Your credit score still runs the show — super-prime borrowers see rates far below those averages, fair-credit borrowers far above. Our car loan guide covers how rates are set and what lenders actually look at.

Let's run the real numbers: $45,000 EV, 7% APR

Time for the part most EV articles skip — the actual payment math. Say you buy a $45,000 EV, put 10% down ($4,500), and finance the remaining $40,500 at 7% APR.

Over 60 months, your payment lands at roughly $802 a month. Total paid over the life of the loan: about $48,114, meaning roughly $7,614 in interest.

Stretch it to 72 months and the payment drops to about $690 a month — a hundred bucks cheaper, feels great. But total interest climbs to roughly $9,215. That $110-a-month "savings" costs you about $1,600 in extra interest. This is the 84-month trap playing out in miniature — and in 2026, with a record quarter of new-car buyers stretching to 84 months or more, it's the single most expensive habit in car buying.

Now layer in the federal interest deduction: on the 60-month loan you're paying roughly $1,500–$2,000 of interest per year in the early years — deductible up to the $10,000 cap if the vehicle qualifies and your income fits. Run your own version with our car loan calculator before you talk to any dealer.

Rule of thumb: if the monthly payment only works on a 72- or 84-month term, the car is too expensive — not the loan too short. Shorter car, shorter term, or bigger down payment.

New vs. used EV: the 2026 plot twist

Here's something that would've sounded crazy a few years ago: the used-EV market might be the best deal in electric cars right now. Used EV sales hit a record in the second quarter of 2026, per Cox Automotive — buyers figured out that a two- or three-year-old EV with most of its battery warranty left can cost dramatically less than new.

The loan math cuts both ways: used-car rates run higher (~11.2% average), but you're borrowing far fewer dollars:

  • Used EV: $26,000 sticker, 15% down, finance $22,100 at 9% APR for 60 months → about $459 a month, roughly $5,426 in total interest.
  • New EV (from above): $40,500 financed at 7% for 60 months → about $802 a month, roughly $7,614 in interest.

The used buyer pays about $343 less monthly and nearly $2,200 less in interest — though the federal interest deduction only applies to new vehicles. The catch with used EVs is the battery: get a battery health report and confirm how much warranty transfers before you buy.

EV depreciation has been brutal — bad for the first owner, great for you. That $45,000 new EV might be $26,000 two years later. Let someone else eat the depreciation.

The running costs your lender never mentions

A loan calculator only sees the loan. But the reason EVs can make sense at today's rates is everything around the loan. Charging at home typically costs a fraction of gasoline per mile — with gas prices elevated through 2026, that gap has only widened. Maintenance is lighter too: no oil changes, no transmission services, brakes that last ages thanks to regenerative braking.

The other side of the ledger: EV insurance tends to run higher (battery packs are expensive to replace after a crash), some states charge annual EV registration surcharges, and public fast-charging costs far more per mile than home charging — which eats into the fuel savings if you can't charge at home.

Estimate your total monthly cost of ownership — payment + insurance + charging − gas savings — not just the payment. An EV at $690 a month that saves you $150 in gas is effectively a $540 car. That's the affordability number, and it's the one our how much car you can afford guide says most buyers get wrong.

How to actually shop the loan (the EV edition)

The process is the same as any car loan, with a few EV-specific twists:

  • Get pre-approved before you visit a dealer. Walk in with a bank or credit union rate and the finance office has to beat it. Without that, you're negotiating blind.
  • Ask about manufacturer promotional APRs first. The automaker's finance arm is often the cheapest money on an EV. But compare the promo rate against any cash rebate offered — sometimes the rebate plus outside financing wins. Run both.
  • Check your state's rebate before you negotiate price. Point-of-sale rebates like Colorado's come off at purchase, which reduces the amount you finance. A $9,000 rebate isn't just $9,000 — it's $9,000 you never pay interest on for six years.
  • Keep the term at 60 months or less if you can. EV tech moves fast — being underwater on a 7-year loan for a car that feels dated in year five is miserable.
  • Document the interest deduction. If you're counting on the federal loan-interest deduction, confirm the vehicle qualifies (new, US-assembled) and keep clean loan records. Tax rules have fine print — this one especially.

The usual wisdom still applies: bigger down payment, no finance-office add-ons rolled into the loan, and never let a payment target push you into a longer term.

Before you sign: get quotes from at least three sources — your bank, a credit union, and the dealer's finance arm. The spread between the best and worst offer on the same car is routinely 2+ percentage points, which is thousands of dollars on an EV-sized loan.

Quick answers

Can I still get a tax credit for buying an EV in 2026?

Not the federal one — the up to $7,500 new and $4,000 used credits ended for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025. What remains federally is the auto loan interest deduction (up to $10,000 of interest per year on a new, US-assembled vehicle financed with a loan, through 2028, subject to income limits). State rebates are still alive in about 20 states, so check yours.

Are EV loan rates higher than gas-car loan rates?

Not inherently — EVs are financed at standard auto loan rates. The average new-car APR was about 7% in late 2026. EVs can actually get better rates when manufacturers run promotional APRs (Tesla offered 1.99% on Model Y in October 2026), which are often the cheapest financing in the market.

Is leasing an EV still a good deal in 2026?

It's weaker than it was. The old leasing loophole that passed a $7,500 federal credit into lease deals is gone, so lease payments now reflect the real cost of the car. Compare the total lease cost against a purchase loan carefully — the math that made EV leases a steal in 2024 doesn't apply anymore.

Should I buy a new or used EV with a loan?

Used is often the better value in 2026: steep EV depreciation means two-year-old cars sell far below sticker, and used EV sales hit records this year. You'll pay a higher loan rate (used-car rates average around 11%), but on a much smaller balance. Just get a battery health report and confirm the remaining battery warranty before committing.

Does the federal loan interest deduction apply to used EVs?

No — it applies to loans on new, US-assembled vehicles, through 2028, with income limits. If you're financing a used EV, you can't claim it, which is one more factor to weigh in the new-vs-used comparison.
