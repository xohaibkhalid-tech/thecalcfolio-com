Compounding Frequency: Does Daily Beat Monthly?

Calculate it for your own numbersFree, no sign-up — runs entirely in your browser.
Interest Calculator →

On $10,000 at 5% for 10 years: annual compounding gives $16,288.95, monthly gives $16,470.09, daily gives $16,486.65. More frequent is better — but the difference between monthly and daily is about $16. Your rate and your time horizon matter far more.

Notice the pattern: each step up helps less than the last. Going from annual to monthly gains $181; going from monthly to daily gains just $16.

So should you chase daily compounding?

Honestly? Don't stress about it. Here's what actually moves your outcome, in order:

  1. Rate — 6% vs 5% over 10 years on $10K is a ~$1,400 difference. Frequency will never do that.
  2. Time — an extra 5 years at 5% adds ~$4,700. Nothing beats time.
  3. Contributions — adding money regularly dwarfs every frequency effect.
  4. Frequency — the $16–$180 we're talking about. Nice, not decisive.

Tip: Where frequency DOES matter: India-style fixed deposits typically compound quarterly — so when estimating FD returns, select 'Quarterly,' not 'Annually,' or you'll undershoot by ~$147 per $10K per decade.

Continuous compounding — the limit

There's a theoretical max: compounding every instant, using e^(rt). On our $10K example that's $16,487.21 — just 56 cents more than daily. The universe of compounding gains is basically exhausted at daily. Good to know, useless to chase.

Quick answers

Which compounding frequency is best for savings?

The most frequent available — but compare APY, not frequency. A 5.12% APY account compounding monthly beats a 5.05% APY account compounding daily.

Do banks really compound daily?

Many do — but plenty of 'daily compounding' accounts pay a lower rate, wiping out the advantage. APY is the only number that settles the argument.

What is continuous compounding?

The mathematical limit as compounding frequency approaches infinity: A = Pe^(rt). Real products don't offer it; it's a textbook concept.
