$100 a month never clears this card: the first month's interest alone is $100, so nothing goes to the balance. Every dollar above the interest is what actually shrinks what you owe — that's why $25 extra a month ($125 instead of $100) turns "never" into 82 months.

How the calculator works

Each month, your card adds interest first, then your payment comes off. The interest is your balance times the monthly rate — APR divided by 12. So on $5,000 at 24% APR, month one adds $100 in interest. A $125 payment covers that $100 and takes $25 off the balance. The next month's interest is worked out on the new, smaller balance, so a little more of each payment goes to principal every month.

To find the payment that clears a balance in a set number of months, the calculator uses the standard loan formula: payment = balance × monthly rate ÷ (1 − (1 + monthly rate)^(−months)). At 0% APR the answer is simply the balance divided by the months.

What it assumes: a fixed APR, no new purchases, no fees, and monthly compounding. Real issuers usually work interest out daily on your average daily balance, so your statement may differ by a few dollars. Your card agreement lists your exact minimum-payment formula — most US cards use 1% of the balance plus that month's interest, with a $25 to $40 floor.

3 ways to pay it off faster

1. Pay a fixed amount, not the minimum

Take your first minimum payment and keep paying that same amount every month, even as the minimum drops. On the $5,000 example above, holding the first minimum of $150 fixed clears the card in 4 years 8 months instead of 19 years 6 months.

2. Move the balance to a 0% card

If your credit qualifies, a balance transfer card stops interest for 12 to 21 months. Check whether the transfer fee is worth it with our balance transfer calculator.

3. Refinance with a fixed-rate loan

A personal loan with a lower rate and a fixed end date can beat a 24% card. Compare the two in personal loan vs credit card, then price it with the personal loan calculator guide.

Credit card payment questions, answered

How much should I pay on my credit card each month?

At least the minimum, always — missing it means late fees and a mark on your credit history. But the minimum is designed to keep you paying for years. A fixed payment you can afford, even a little above the minimum, cuts years and thousands in interest. Use the calculator above to find a payment that fits your budget and your deadline.

How long will it take to pay off my credit card?

It depends on your balance, your APR and your monthly payment. A $5,000 balance at 24% APR takes 82 months at $125 a month, 36 months at $200 a month, and 234 months if you only ever pay the minimum. Enter your own numbers above for your exact payoff date.

How can I pay off my credit card faster?

Pay the same fixed amount every month instead of the shrinking minimum, add any spare cash to the payment, stop new purchases on the card while you pay it down, and look at a 0% balance transfer or a lower-rate personal loan if your credit allows it.

Is it better to pay a fixed amount or only the minimum?

A fixed amount, by a wide margin. On a $5,000 balance at 24% APR, a fixed $200 a month saves $6,886 in interest and 16 years 6 months of payments compared with paying only the minimum.

What monthly payment clears my balance in 3 years?

Use the "I have a deadline" mode above and set 36 months. On $5,000 at 24% APR the answer is $197 a month. US statements must show a similar 3-year payment box — the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains what that 3-year box on your statement means.

Does paying more than the minimum help my credit score?

Yes. Paying more lowers your balance faster, which lowers your credit utilization — one of the biggest factors in your score. Paying on time, even just the minimum, protects your payment history.

What if my payment doesn't cover the monthly interest?

Then the balance grows instead of shrinking, and you never pay the card off. A $100 payment on a $5,000 balance at 24% APR covers exactly the $100 of interest in month one — nothing goes to the balance. Pay more than the monthly interest, or move the balance to a 0% card, to start making progress.
