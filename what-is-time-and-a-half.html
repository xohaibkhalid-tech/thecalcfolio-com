What Is Time and a Half? Overtime Pay, Explained

Calculate it for your own numbersFree, no sign-up — runs entirely in your browser.

Time and a half means 1.5 times your regular hourly rate. Earn $20/hour? Your overtime rate is $30/hour. It's the federal minimum for overtime under the FLSA — and the number behind almost every overtime paycheck in America.

The math (it's simple)

Overtime rate = hourly rate × 1.5

When does it kick in?

Under federal law: hours over 40 in a workweek, for non-exempt employees. Work 45 hours at $20/hour?

  • Regular: 40 × $20 = $800
  • Overtime: 5 × $30 = $150
  • Total: $950

Watch your "regular rate." Legally, the 1.5x applies to your regular rate — which includes non-discretionary bonuses, shift differentials, and commissions, not just your base hourly number. If your employer calculates OT on base pay alone while you earn bonuses, you're likely being underpaid.

Time and a half vs. double time

Double time (2x) is rarer — California requires it after 12 hours in a day, and some union contracts include it. Everything else in the US runs on time and a half. If someone offers you "straight time" for overtime hours, that's below the federal minimum for covered employees.

Quick answers

Does time and a half apply to salaried workers?

Sometimes. Salaried doesn't automatically mean exempt — it depends on your job duties and salary level under FLSA tests. Many salaried workers are misclassified; if your duties are non-exempt work, you may be owed overtime regardless of salary.

Is time and a half required on holidays?

Federal law doesn't require it — holiday premium pay is up to your employer or contract. But if holiday hours push you over 40 for the week, those hours still earn time and a half under normal OT rules.

What if I work two jobs?

Each employer calculates separately. Hours at job A don't combine with hours at job B for overtime — unless the two employers are joint employers, which is a legal question, not a math one.

General information only, not legal advice. Overtime eligibility depends on your classification, state, and employer. Consult the Department of Labor or an employment attorney about your situation.
