Can Your Employer Refuse to Pay Overtime?

Updated October 2026 · 5 min read

For non-exempt employees: no. If you worked the hours, your employer owes you overtime pay — even if they told you not to work overtime. They can discipline or fire you for working unauthorized hours in most states, but they still have to pay for the hours you worked.

The rule most people get backwards

There's a common belief: "my boss said no overtime, so those extra 3 hours don't count." Wrong. Under the FLSA, hours worked = hours paid. An employer's policy can't override federal wage law.

What the employer can do in most states: write you up, or even fire you, for violating the no-overtime policy. The law protects your pay, not your job. (Some states and union contracts add protections — check yours.)

Tricks that don't work

  • "You're salaried, so no overtime." Salary doesn't decide — duties and salary level do. Misclassification is rampant.
  • "We'll give you comp time instead." Private-sector employers generally cannot substitute comp time for overtime pay. (Government employers can, under specific rules.)
  • "You're an independent contractor." If the company controls your schedule and work, you may be an employee in the eyes of the law regardless of the label.
  • Averaging across weeks — "you did 50 this week and 30 last week, so it evens out." Illegal. Each workweek stands alone.

Tip: Keep your own records. Screenshots of schedules, time-clock photos, messages asking you to stay late — if a dispute ever happens, your notes beat their memory.

What to do if you're not being paid

  1. Raise it in writing (email counts) — polite, factual, with dates and hours.
  2. If that fails, file a complaint with the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division — it's free, and retaliation for filing is illegal.
  3. Your state labor agency may have stronger rules (California's daily overtime, for example).
  4. For larger amounts, an employment attorney's consultation is often free.

Quick answers

Can I be fired for working overtime without permission?

In most at-will states, yes — but you must still be paid for those hours. The firing can be legal while the non-payment is not.

Does my employer have to approve overtime in advance?

They can require approval as a policy, and punish violations — but approval is not a precondition for the pay itself under federal law.

What if I'm paid a day rate or piece rate?

You're still likely entitled to overtime. The regular rate gets calculated from your total earnings divided by hours worked, then the half-time premium applies.

General information only, not legal advice. Wage law is fact-specific — consult the Department of Labor or an employment attorney.
