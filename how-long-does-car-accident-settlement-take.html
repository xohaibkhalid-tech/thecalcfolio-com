How Long Does a Car Accident Settlement Take?

Updated October 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer: a straightforward case can settle in 3–6 months. A typical case takes 6–12 months. A disputed or serious-injury case can run 1–2 years or more — especially if it goes to trial. Nobody can give you an exact date, but the stages below explain where the time actually goes.

If you're waiting on a settlement check and refreshing your email every morning, this guide is for you. Not legal advice — just an honest map of the process, so you know what's normal and what's a red flag.

The 5 stages (and where the time goes)

1. Medical treatment & recoveryweeks – months

This is usually the longest stage, and it's mostly out of everyone's control. Your attorney generally won't settle until you've reached maximum medical improvement (MMI) — the point where doctors know your final condition. Settling before that means guessing at future medical costs, which is how people leave money on the table.

2. Demand letter & negotiation1–3 months

Once treatment wraps up, your lawyer sends a demand package to the insurer: medical records, bills, lost-wage documentation. The insurer responds with a low offer (almost always), and negotiation begins. Simple, clear-liability cases often settle right here.

3. Filing a lawsuitadds months

If negotiation stalls, your attorney files suit. This doesn't mean you're going to trial — the majority of filed cases still settle. But filing restarts the clock on a formal timeline and signals you're serious, which often moves the insurer.

4. Discovery6–12 months

Both sides exchange evidence: depositions, medical exams, documents. This is the grind phase. It's slow by design — and it's where most of the "my case is taking forever" frustration lives.

5. Mediation or triala day – weeks

Many courts require mediation before trial, and a large share of cases settle there in a single day. If yours actually reaches trial, add more months of waiting for a court date — trial calendars are crowded.

What makes a case slower (or faster)

  • Disputed liability — if the other side says the crash was your fault, expect months of argument before numbers are even discussed.
  • Serious or unclear injuries — bigger medical pictures take longer to document and are worth more, so insurers fight harder.
  • Multiple parties — more insurers at the table means more negotiation rounds.
  • Low policy limits — ironically, these can speed things up: when the at-fault driver's coverage maxes out early, there's less to argue about.
  • Your state's court backlog — filing suit in a congested county adds real calendar time.

What you can do to avoid slowing it down

The biggest self-inflicted delay: gaps in medical treatment. Miss appointments, skip physical therapy for a month, and the insurer will argue you weren't really hurt — adding months of dispute. Follow your treatment plan, keep every receipt, and send everything to your attorney promptly.

  • Don't give recorded statements to the other insurer without your lawyer.
  • Don't post about the crash or your injuries on social media.
  • Respond to your attorney's requests quickly — your file can't move while it waits on you.

Quick answers

Can I speed up my settlement?

You can't rush medical recovery or court calendars — but you can avoid slowing things down: complete treatment without gaps, document everything, and respond to your lawyer fast. An organized client is a faster case.

Why do lawyers wait so long to settle?

Usually it's strategic, not lazy. Settling before your medical picture is complete means the settlement can't cover treatment you haven't had yet. Patience here is often worth real money.

Do most car accident cases go to trial?

No — the overwhelming majority settle out of court. Filing a lawsuit and going to trial are very different things; most filed cases settle during discovery or at mediation.

What if I need money now?

Some companies offer advances against pending settlements ("settlement loans"), but they carry very high fees — treat them as a last resort and read every term. A faster option is often just asking your attorney for a realistic timeline so you can plan.

This article is general information, not legal advice. Settlement timelines vary by state, injury, and insurer. Consult a licensed attorney in your state about your specific case.
