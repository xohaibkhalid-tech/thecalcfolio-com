Why time matters more than rate

Interest earns interest. In the example above, the first year adds about $240 of interest; the tenth year adds more than $1,300, on the same $200 a month. Starting early, or simply not withdrawing, does more than chasing an extra quarter-point of APY.

That said, APYs vary a lot between banks. On a $20,000 balance, the difference between 0.5% and 4% is about $700 a year. If your savings sit in a low-rate account at a big bank, moving them is one of the easiest raises you'll ever get. Our guide to what counts as a good APY explains how to compare.

Frequently asked questions

How much interest will I earn on my savings?
Multiply your balance by the APY for a rough yearly figure: $10,000 at 4% APY earns about $400 in a year. Regular deposits add more. $5,000 plus $200 a month at 4% APY grows to about $36,740 in 10 years, of which about $7,740 is interest.
How is savings interest calculated?
Most savings accounts compound daily or monthly and quote the result as APY. This calculator converts APY to an equivalent monthly rate, adds interest to the balance each month and adds your deposit at the end of each month.
Is APY the same as the interest rate?
No. APY includes compounding, so it's the better number for comparing accounts. Two accounts with the same interest rate can have slightly different APYs if one compounds more often.
Is interest on savings taxable?
In the US, yes. Savings interest is generally taxed as ordinary income in the year it's paid, and banks send a 1099-INT when you earn $10 or more. Interest inside tax-advantaged accounts like a 401(k) or IRA follows those accounts' rules.
Savings account or CD?
A savings account lets you withdraw any time but its rate can change; a CD locks in a rate for a set term with a penalty for early withdrawal. Many people keep an emergency fund in savings and longer-term money in CDs.
