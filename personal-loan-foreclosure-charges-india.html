Personal Loan Foreclosure in India: Is Prepaying Worth It?

Short answer: prepaying a personal loan usually saves you real money — and since 1 January 2026, the RBI has banned foreclosure charges on floating-rate personal loans, so closing early costs you nothing extra. The real question isn't whether prepaying works. It's whether that cash could do better elsewhere.

The rule change nobody told you about

Until recently, closing your personal loan early came with a sting. Banks and NBFCs routinely charged 2–4% of the outstanding amount as a "foreclosure charge" or "prepayment penalty." On a ₹4 lakh outstanding balance, a 3% charge meant handing over ₹12,000 just for the privilege of giving the bank its own money back early. Absurd, but that was the game.

That game is over — at least for most borrowers. From 1 January 2026, under the RBI's pre-payment directions, regulated lenders cannot levy any prepayment or foreclosure charge on floating-rate loans to individuals. Personal loans are included. You can now prepay any amount, at any time, from any source, completely free of charge.

Now the fine print, because there always is some. The ban applies to floating-rate loans sanctioned or renewed on or after 1 January 2026. Fixed-rate loans can still carry foreclosure clauses as per the lender's policy. If your loan was sanctioned before the cut-off date, the old terms in your agreement still apply — but refinancing or renewing after the date brings you under the new rule.

Why does this matter right now? Because in October 2026 the RBI hiked the repo rate to 5.50% and signalled a "calibrated tightening" stance — meaning floating rates are more likely to creep up than fall. Every month you carry a floating-rate personal loan in a rising-rate phase, your total interest bill grows. And now, for the first time, escaping that bill early costs you nothing.

Check your loan type first: look at your sanction letter or the RBI-mandated Key Facts Statement. If it says "floating rate" and was sanctioned after 1 Jan 2026, your foreclosure charge is zero by law. If it says "fixed," read the foreclosure clause carefully.

Why prepaying early in the loan saves the most

Here's the thing most borrowers miss: loan interest is front-loaded. In the early years of a loan, most of each EMI goes toward interest and only a small slice touches the principal. That means every rupee of prepayment in year one or two kills off principal that would otherwise have generated interest for years.

Prepay in the final year of the loan and you'll barely feel a difference — most of the interest is already paid. Prepay in the first year and you can wipe out lakhs of future interest. Timing is everything. Our guide on how loan amortization works shows exactly how this front-loading plays out month by month.

Honestly, this is why the RBI's zero-charge rule is such a big deal for personal loans specifically. Home loan foreclosure charges were already banned years ago. Personal loan borrowers were the ones getting squeezed — and personal loan rates (11–24%) are far higher than home loan rates, so the savings from prepaying are proportionally bigger.

Let's run the real numbers

Say you took a ₹5 lakh personal loan at 13% for 5 years. Your EMI works out to roughly ₹11,378 a month.

Two years in, you've paid 24 EMIs. Your outstanding balance is about ₹337,600. Now suppose a bonus lands and you decide to foreclose the whole thing. What's the saving?

If you keep paying normally, you'd hand over 36 more EMIs: 36 × ₹11,378 = about ₹409,600. Foreclose now and you pay ₹337,600 once. The difference — roughly ₹72,000 — is interest you'd never pay. That's the prize.

Under the old regime, a 4% foreclosure charge would have shaved about ₹13,500 off that prize. Under the new RBI rule, if your loan qualifies, that charge is zero. The full ₹72,000 stays in your pocket.

Don't have the full amount? A partial prepayment works the same way, just smaller. Drop ₹1 lakh against the principal at the two-year mark and the future interest saved is close to ₹21,000 — again, with zero penalty now. Always reduce tenure, not EMI: when you make a partial prepayment, ask the bank to keep your EMI the same and shorten the loan instead. Cutting tenure multiplies your interest saving; cutting EMI just gives you pocket money now.

When prepaying is a bad idea

Prepaying isn't automatically the right move. Be honest with yourself about these cases:

  • Your loan is fixed-rate with a foreclosure clause. The RBI ban doesn't cover fixed-rate loans. If your lender charges 3% and you've only got a year left, the math may not work. Calculate the charge against the interest you'd save — our guide on calculating loan interest helps you do this cleanly.
  • Your money earns more elsewhere. If ₹3 lakh in your business or investments consistently earns more than the 13% your loan costs, the loan can wait. Idle cash earning 6–7% in savings while a 13% loan runs? That one should be prepaid.
  • You'd wipe out your emergency fund. Never drain your safety net to close a loan. That's trading a manageable cost for a dangerous risk — one hospital bill and you're borrowing again at worse terms.
  • You're building credit history. If your CIBIL score is low and this loan is your main credit line, a track record of perfect EMIs can lift your score. Closing it isn't always a credit win.

One more thing in your favour: unlike home loans, personal loans give you no tax benefit on interest. So prepaying a personal loan never costs you a tax deduction. One less thing to weigh.

The smart way to do it

If the math says go, do it properly:

  1. Confirm the zero charge in writing. Get the bank to confirm — by email or in the foreclosure letter — that no prepayment charge applies. Rules are rules, but paperwork wins arguments.
  2. Prepay early in the loan's life. The earlier the prepayment, the bigger the interest saved. A bonus in year one beats the same bonus in year four.
  3. Choose tenure reduction over EMI reduction for partial prepayments, as explained above.
  4. Collect the closure documents. Get a loan closure letter / NOC and make sure the bank reports the closure to CIBIL. Check your credit report a month or two later to confirm it shows "closed," not "settled."
  5. Redirect the EMI. The month after closure, redirect that ₹11,378 into an SIP or your emergency fund. You were already living without that money — make it work for you now.

Quick answers

Does foreclosing a personal loan hurt my CIBIL score?

Not really. Your score might dip slightly when an active account closes, but lower overall debt and a "closed" status on your report are positive long-term. Just make sure the bank reports it as closed, not settled.

Can my bank refuse my prepayment?

For floating-rate loans, no — that's exactly what the RBI's 2026 directions protect. Some lenders have minimum prepayment amounts or ask for a few days' notice, which is fine. Fixed-rate loans may still have lock-in periods, so check your agreement.

With the October 2026 repo hike, should I prepay now?

If your personal loan is floating-rate, a rising-rate environment strengthens the case for prepaying — every month you wait, your total interest exposure grows. The RBI's calibrated tightening stance suggests more hikes or a long pause, not cuts.

Is there a minimum amount for partial prepayment?

Most lenders accept partial prepayments of any reasonable size, though some set a minimum (often ₹10,000–₹25,000 or a few EMIs' worth). Ask your bank's specific rule before transferring.

My loan was taken in 2024. Do I get the zero-charge benefit?

Only if the loan is renewed or refinanced after 1 January 2026. The terms in your original 2024 agreement still apply otherwise. If your old loan carries a foreclosure charge, refinancing into a new floating-rate loan can both get you the zero-charge rule and possibly a better rate.
