Offset Mortgage vs Overpayments: Which One Actually Saves You More?

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

An offset mortgage makes your savings work against your mortgage: £30,000 of savings on a £220,000 mortgage at 5.99% saves you about £1,800 a year in interest — better than the ~£1,260 those savings would earn in a typical account. Overpaying saves more pound-for-pound but locks your cash away. If you need access to your money, offset wins. If you never want to touch it again, overpaying wins. The maths favours whichever one matches your life.

The weirdest arrangement in British finance

Think about it. You have a mortgage charging you, say, 6%. You also have £30,000 sitting in a savings account earning 4.2% — and then HMRC takes a slice of that. So you're paying 6% on one side of the ledger while earning maybe 2.5% after tax on the other. The two accounts often sit in the same banking app, on the same screen, doing absolutely nothing for each other. It's a strange way to run your money, and millions of households do it without a second thought.

An offset mortgage fixes this by linking the two. Your savings sit in a separate account, still yours, still accessible — but the lender charges mortgage interest only on the difference between your balance and your savings. £220,000 mortgage, £30,000 savings, and you pay interest as though you owed £190,000. You haven't repaid a penny of the loan, and your cash is still there if you need it.

Offsetting went out of fashion when savings paid almost nothing and mortgage rates were on the floor — the gap it exploits was too narrow to matter. That gap has reopened with a bang. With the Bank of England base rate at 3.75%, typical fixed mortgage rates near 6%, and easy-access savings around 4.2%, offsetting suddenly makes serious sense again. Advisers are writing about it, lenders are pushing it, and for households sitting on decent savings, it's genuinely worth a hard look.

The worked example: £220,000 mortgage, £30,000 savings

Let's put real numbers on both strategies. You owe £220,000 at 5.99% on a 25-year fix. You have £30,000 in savings. Here are your three options:

Option A: Leave the savings alone. Your £30,000 earns 4.2% in an easy-access account: about £1,260 a year before tax. If you're a basic-rate taxpayer with no personal savings allowance left, that's more like £1,008 after tax. Meanwhile your £220,000 mortgage accrues roughly £13,180 in interest in year one. Net position: you're paying about £12,170 in interest after accounting for the savings.

Option B: Offset the £30,000. You now pay interest on £190,000 instead of £220,000. Year-one interest drops to roughly £11,385. That single change saves you about £1,795 a year — tax-free, because it's interest you never paid rather than interest you earned. Compared with the £1,008–£1,260 your savings would have earned, offsetting is ahead by £535 to £787 a year. And your £30,000 is still sitting there, untouched, available for emergencies or that extension you've been planning.

Option C: Overpay the £30,000 as a lump sum. Your balance drops to £190,000 — the same interest saving as the offset, about £1,795 a year. Pound-for-pound, identical. The difference is access: that £30,000 is gone from your savings. Most lenders won't let you borrow it back. If your boiler dies in January, you're reaching for a credit card instead of your own money.

So the maths says: offset and overpaying save the same interest, but offset keeps your cash liquid. That liquidity is worth real money when life happens.

The tax angle nobody mentions: offset savings are effectively tax-free, because you're avoiding interest rather than earning it. For higher-rate taxpayers paying 40% on savings interest, £30,000 in a 4.2% account nets only £756 a year — less than half what offsetting the same money saves. The higher your tax bracket, the more offsetting wins.

When overpaying beats offsetting anyway

Offset isn't automatically better. There are plenty of situations where a plain overpayment on a standard mortgage wins:

  • You don't have meaningful savings. Offset mortgages only help if you actually have cash to offset. If your spare money is a few hundred quid a month of cash flow, monthly overpayments on a normal mortgage do the same job more simply.
  • The offset deal is pricier. Offset mortgages often carry slightly higher rates or fees than the cheapest standard fixes — maybe 0.1 to 0.3 percentage points. On a £220,000 mortgage, a 0.2% premium costs £440 a year. If your savings are small, that premium can wipe out the offset benefit entirely. Do the maths for your own numbers before falling in love with the concept.
  • You'd raid the savings. This is the honest one. Offset savings stay accessible — which means they're accessible to you on a bad day, a weak moment, or a shiny car advert. An overpayment is a commitment device: the money's gone, working hard, and you can't easily undo it. Some of us need that. Know yourself.
  • You want the simplest possible life. One mortgage, one standing order, done. Offset products add moving parts: linked accounts, minimum savings balances, sometimes quirks around how payments are applied. Simplicity has a value that doesn't show up in a spreadsheet.

If you're mid-deal and just want to chip away, our mortgage overpayment guide covers the habit side. Who offset mortgages are really for

After all the maths, the profile of someone who should seriously consider an offset is pretty specific:

Self-employed people and contractors are the classic case. Irregular income means you need a fat buffer — maybe six months of expenses sitting in cash. On a standard mortgage that buffer earns 4% while your mortgage charges 6%. Offset turns that dead buffer into a 6%-equivalent return, tax-free, with the money still there when a client pays late.

Parents saving for school fees or university are another. You've got £40,000 earmarked for the next three years of fees. You can't overpay it into the mortgage because you'll need it back. Offset lets that money work at mortgage-rate levels until the fees are due.

Anyone sitting on a house-sale deposit for their next move. Waiting to buy, funds parked for six months? Offset them against your current mortgage in the meantime. Just check the deal's terms — some offset products restrict when you can move money.

The flip side: if you're a first-time buyer scraping together every penny for the deposit, or your savings are thin, a bog-standard cheap fix plus regular overpayments is the better play. Offset is a tool for people who have money and want it working harder — not a trick for people who don't.

Quick answers

Do offset mortgages cost more than standard mortgages?

Slightly, usually. Offset products tend to price 0.1 to 0.3 percentage points above the cheapest equivalent standard fix, and arrangement fees can be higher. The offset benefit has to beat that premium — with large savings it easily does; with small savings it might not. Run your own numbers rather than assuming.

Can I still overpay on an offset mortgage?

Yes. Most offset mortgages let you make overpayments on top of the offsetting, usually within the standard 10% annual allowance. The two strategies stack — the offset handles your lump savings, overpayments handle your monthly surplus.

What happens to my offset savings if house prices fall?

Nothing. Your savings are separate from the property — the offset only affects how interest is calculated. Even if you're in negative equity, the offset keeps working. (Though if you're remortgaging in negative equity, that's a separate problem.)

Is offsetting better than an ISA for my savings?

Often, yes, for higher-rate taxpayers. A cash ISA shelters the interest from tax, but the best cash ISA rates are still usually below mortgage rates. At a 6% mortgage rate versus a 4.5% ISA, offsetting your savings wins — unless you need the ISA's specific features or you're already using your ISA allowance strategically.

Can I get an offset mortgage as a first-time buyer?

You can, but it rarely makes sense. First-time buyers typically have minimal savings left after the deposit, and the slightly higher offset rate means you'd pay more for a feature you can't use yet. A cheap standard fix with regular overpayments is usually the smarter start.
